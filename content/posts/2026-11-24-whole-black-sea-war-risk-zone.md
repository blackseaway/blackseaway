On 16 September 2026 the Joint War Committee (JWC), which advises the London marine insurance market, issued circular JWLA-035 and extended the Black Sea "Listed Area" to the whole basin. Only the territorial waters of Türkiye, Romania, Bulgaria and Georgia stay outside it. Until then, the listing covered mainly Russian and Ukrainian waters and the north-western part of the sea.

For anyone moving goods by sea this is more than a line on a map. A voyage into a listed area has to be declared to war-risk insurers in advance, and insurers can then set additional premiums and conditions. Gard, one of the largest P&I clubs, advises ships to consult their war-risk insurer before entering the Black Sea at all.

## Why the insurers moved

The numbers collected by the maritime security company Ambrey explain the decision:

- **The attacks moved offshore.** In the 12 months to 21 September 2026, 45 merchant ships were struck outside the old listed area. In the previous 12 months there were none. 33 of those strikes happened in the last three months of that period.
- **The intensity rose sharply.** Inside the old listed area, strikes on merchant ships went from an average of 4 per month in the fourth year of the war to 28 per month in the fifth.
- **The targets are far from shore.** Ukraine-attributed strikes on Russia-linked shipping took place at a median of about 58 nautical miles from the nearest coast, concentrated off northern Türkiye and south of Novorossiysk and Sochi.
- **Ports are hit more often.** Strikes on ports rose from 21 to 139 from one quarter to the next.

The campaign against Russia-linked ships began in July 2026 with tankers. According to Ambrey, the risk has since spread to bulk carriers, general cargo ships, container ships and ro-ro vessels, and targeting depends on a ship's sanctions exposure, ownership and trading history, not only on its flag or declared destination. Russian forces, for their part, continue to strike ships bound for Ukrainian ports: on 23 September an Antigua and Barbuda-flagged cargo ship approaching Odesa was hit, and its Ukrainian master was killed.

## Drones have replaced mines as the main floating hazard

In the first year of the war almost every piece of unexploded ordnance found at sea was a mine: 15 out of 17. This year only 2 of the 53 objects found were mines. The rest were aerial and surface drones. Finds outside Russian and Ukrainian waters rose from 11 to 65 year on year, and the highest density is along the Romanian, Bulgarian and Turkish coasts.

## "Excluded" does not mean "safe"

The coastal waters of Romania, Bulgaria, Türkiye and Georgia are outside the listing, but they are not outside the war. A tanker was struck about 11.6 nautical miles north of the Bosphorus, and several ships were hit in October and November off Bulgaria and near the Sulina canal in Romania. Ambrey expects the open Black Sea to remain contested for the foreseeable future, because neither side accepts a maritime truce.

## What ship operators and cargo owners should do now

Based on guidance from the NATO Shipping Centre, the U.S. Maritime Administration and Gard:

1. **Talk to your war-risk insurer before fixing a Black Sea voyage,** including calls at Romanian, Bulgarian and Turkish ports, and check the notification requirements in your policy.
2. **Keep AIS on and monitor VHF channel 16.** AIS may be switched off only if it compromises safety; the reason must be logged and AIS turned back on as soon as possible.
3. **Expect GNSS interference,** especially along the northern and western coasts, and cross-check positions by other means.
4. **Keep a sharp lookout for floating objects,** keep the forward deck clear of crew where practicable, stay well clear of anything that looks like a mine or drone, and report it.
5. **Report any incident or suspicious activity** to the flag state, the coastal authorities and the NATO Shipping Centre.
6. **Check current navigational warnings** (NAVAREA III and national NAVTEX) before and during the voyage. Links are on our home page.
